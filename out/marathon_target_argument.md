# Argument: NYC Marathon (Nov 1, 2026) target should be ~3:25 (7:49/mi, 4:51/km)

**Recommendation.** Target 3:25. Treat 3:20 (7:38/mi) as a stretch goal, only if an 18–20 mi long run goes well in the next two weeks. Expect a bad-day floor of 3:35–3:40. This is the runner's first marathon.

Please argue against this with evidence if you disagree. The specific claims are below.

---

## 1. Data used
- **Source.** 188 COROS runs (Aug 2025 – Sep 2026), analyzed from per-second FIT files. The analysis used grade-adjusted pace, heat-adjusted heart rate (NOAA air temperature and dew point), cardiac drift and decoupling, and interval detection.
- **Heart-rate anchors.** HRmax about 197, as reported by the runner; the highest seen in recent races is 189. Resting HR about 45 (from Whoop).
- **Recent races** (both HR-confirmed as race-level efforts):
  - **Half marathon, 2025-10-18:** 1:30:01 at 13 °C. Even/negative split (4:18 → 4:14/km per quarter), average HR 90% of max. **VDOT 51.0.**
  - **10k, 2026-08-29:** 41:27 at 22 °C, dew point 13 °C. Went out too fast (3:57 → 4:21/km by quarter), average HR 93% of max. **VDOT 49.8.**
- **Current fitness.** The HR-based VO2max index is 54.8 and rising, up from 53.7 at the August 10k. Calibrated to the two races, that is **VDOT ≈ 51.**
- **Training volume:**
  - Last 8 weeks: average **~30 mi/week** at 5:55/km.
  - Recent weeks: 32, 35, 38, 27 mi.
  - Planned peak: 42 and 44 mi this week and next, then a taper.
  - Projected 8-week average before the race (Tanda's window): **~35 mi/week.**
- **Longest runs:** 24.4 km (15.2 mi) on Sep 20, 22.6 km on Sep 13, 21.1 km on Sep 6. **No run of 16 mi or more yet.**
- **Aerobic durability.** Decoupling on steady long runs is mostly under 5%, with low drift in bpm/hour. That is good, but it was measured at easy pace (~5:50/km, ~75% HRmax) over 2–2.4 h, not at marathon pace over 3.5 h.

## 2. Three prediction methods disagree
| Method | Prediction | Main bias |
|---|---|---|
| Race fitness, Daniels VDOT 51 converted to the marathon | **3:07–3:08** (flat course) | Assumes the runner is fully marathon-trained. Too optimistic for low-mileage and first-time marathoners. |
| Same, corrected for recreational runners (+≥10 min) and the NYC course (+4–5 min elite, more for recreational runners) | **~3:15–3:22** | Still assumes average durability for that fitness level. |
| Tanda (2011): 8-week mean km/week + mean training pace | **~3:43–3:46** | Easy days are slow relative to race fitness, which drags this down. Likely somewhat pessimistic for this runner. |

**3:25 sits between the corrected race-based estimate and the training-based one, closer to the race side.** The reasons follow.

## 3. Why not faster (≤3:15)
1. **Race-based conversions break at the marathon for recreational runners.** Vickers & Vertosick (2016, n=2,303) found Riegel's formula well-calibrated up to the half marathon. For the marathon it was **10+ minutes too fast for half of runners**. Weekly mileage was the strongest single predictor; in their data, going from 30 to 50 mi/week was worth about 25 minutes for men. This runner is at the low end, around 30–35 mi/week.
2. **No long run of 16 mi or more.** The final 10 km is where low mileage and short long runs show up. Fitness measured over 41–90 minute races says little about minutes 150–210.
3. **NYC is slower than flat courses.** It has about 300 m of climbing over five bridges, including the Verrazano at mile 1, the Queensboro at mile 15–16 and Fifth Avenue at mile 23. It also has 26 turns. Elites average about 4 minutes slower than their PBs there; a course guide says recreational runners lose 5–10 minutes compared with Berlin or Chicago.
4. **Pacing risk.** The runner's most recent race went out about 4% too fast and faded about 10%. In a marathon, an early surplus costs far more than it saves.
5. **The costs are lopsided.** Starting 10 seconds/mile too slow costs about 4 minutes at most and can partly be recovered after mile 16. Starting too fast and hitting the wall at miles 20–23 commonly costs 10–20+ minutes.

## 4. Why not slower (≥3:40)
1. **Tanda likely underrates this runner.** He runs easy days slowly (5:55/km) relative to race fitness; Daniels' easy range for VDOT 51 is roughly 5:10–5:45/km. Tanda gives that mean training pace a heavy weight.
2. **Races beat the HR model.** Both races came in about 7% faster than the HR-based fitness model predicts (HM 1:30:01 vs 1:37:26 predicted; 10k 41:27 vs 44:14). That points to good economy and race execution.
3. **Fitness is rising.** The index is up about 2% since August, and volume is increasing into peak weeks.
4. **Aerobic durability is good.** Low decoupling on 2+ hour runs suggests the aerobic base is not the limiter.
5. **The weather should help.** Early-November NYC is usually near the 10k/marathon temperature optimum of about 7.5–15 °C WBGT. Performance drops only about 0.3–0.4% per °C WBGT outside that range.

## 5. Execution plan that goes with the target
- **Miles 1–13:** about 7:55–8:00/mi. Run the Verrazano climb by effort, and don't bank time on the mile 2 descent.
- **Miles 13–16:** settle to about 7:49 goal pace. Take the Queensboro Bridge by effort.
- **Miles 16–20, First Avenue:** speed up only if you feel genuinely good.
- **Miles 23–26.2:** expect to lose time on the Fifth Avenue climb and the Central Park rollers. Hold effort, not pace.
- **Stretch-goal condition:** move the target to 3:20 only if an 18–20 mi long run in the next two weeks finishes comfortably, with the last 3–4 mi near 7:45–7:50/mi and HR under ~170.

## 6. Known uncertainties (stated honestly)
- The fitness calibration rests on two races. They agree closely: ratios of 0.931 and 0.928.
- HRmax is self-reported at 197; the highest seen in recent races is 189. Each ±3 bpm moves the index about ±1, which moves marathon predictions by about ±3 minutes.
- First-marathon variance is large. ±10 minutes around any point estimate is realistic.
- The HR data comes from an optical wrist sensor. Cadence lock was filtered, but some error remains.
